In 2024, Anthropic ran an experiment called **Project Fetch**: two human teams competed to teach a robot dog to fetch a ball. One team had Claude assistance; one didn't. The AI-assisted team won — but only because the model was helping humans move faster. The model couldn't complete the task autonomously. It failed as early as the **robot connection step**.

Two years later, the follow-up — **Project Fetch Phase Two** — tells a fundamentally different story.

**Claude Opus 4.7 completed the entire task — autonomously.** Connecting to the robot. Reading sensors and cameras. Writing control code. Training the dog to fetch. **No human help.** Approximately **20x faster** than the best human team from the previous experiment.

This is a capability jump that crosses a meaningful threshold. The qualitative shift isn't "AI helped humans move faster." It's **"AI taught another autonomous physical system to do something."** That's a different category.

## What "Project Fetch" Actually Tests

The benchmark is deliberately constructed to probe end-to-end embodied agency. To succeed, a model has to:

1. **Connect** to a physical robot (handle drivers, protocols, hardware abstraction)
2. **Sense** the environment (read sensors, process camera feeds)
3. **Plan** the task (decompose "fetch the ball" into motion primitives)
4. **Write code** to control the robot (generate executable behavior)
5. **Iterate** on failures (debug when the dog drops the ball, when sensing is noisy, when physics behaves unexpectedly)
6. **Achieve the goal** within reasonable time and reliability

In 2024, Claude got through step 1 with human help and gave up at step 2. In 2026, Opus 4.7 completes all six — **without intervention**.

## The 20x Speedup Is the Story

Speed is the metric that breaks the narrative. A 20x improvement over the best human team means the AI is doing something that isn't just "good enough" — it's **structurally faster** at the loop of:

> sense → plan → act → observe → re-plan

Humans are slow at this loop because we get tired, distracted, and have to context-switch between tools. The AI is fast because it can iterate on failure modes at the rate a model can produce tokens. Twenty times faster over two years is roughly an order of magnitude per year — consistent with the broader capability curve.

What 20x buys you in practice:

- **Training time collapses from days to hours.** Tasks that took a human team weeks of debugging now happen in a single afternoon.
- **Iteration density goes up.** The AI can try 20 different control strategies in the time a human team tries one.
- **Failure modes get explored faster.** The "what happens when the dog drops the ball?" branch gets visited hundreds of times before the human equivalent reaches it once.

## What This Means for Embodied AI

The traditional narrative around humanoid robotics has been: **"AI helps humans control robots."** That's a useful category — copilots, teleoperation assistance, code generation for control software. Project Fetch Phase Two is something else: **"AI controls robots end-to-end without humans in the loop."**

That's the same distinction that distinguishes Level 2 from Level 4 autonomous driving. Level 2 requires a human ready to take over. Level 4 doesn't. The robot dog task crossed Level 4 for a narrow but real domain in 2026.

The categories that are now within reach:

- **Industrial inspection:** Robots that autonomously learn to navigate new environments without pre-mapping.
- **Warehouse picking:** End-to-end manipulation tasks where the robot writes its own grasp code based on what it sees.
- **Field robotics:** Agricultural, mining, construction robots that adapt to unstructured environments in real time.

None of these are fully solved yet. But the **rate** at which they're becoming solvable just changed.

## The Caveats Worth Naming

Project Fetch is a controlled experiment with a known hardware target and a known task. Real-world deployment is messier:

- **Distribution shift:** The AI was tested on one robot in one environment. New robots, new lighting, new tasks — generalization is the open question.
- **Safety:** An AI writing its own control code in real time raises failure mode questions that a pre-scripted controller doesn't.
- **Reliability over time:** A benchmark success is a single run. Industrial deployment needs consistent success over thousands of hours.
- **Cost:** Running Opus 4.7 in the loop is more expensive than running a scripted controller. The economics matter.

These are the reasons this won't immediately replace every robotics engineer. But they're exactly the kinds of problems that compound capability improvements solve — and 20x in two years is the kind of improvement that compounds.

## The Takeaway

The capability jump from "AI assists a human teaching a robot" in 2024 to "AI autonomously teaches a robot" in 2026 is the kind of curve that, if continued, puts general-purpose embodied agency within a decade.

The robot dog benchmark is narrow. But narrow benchmarks historically generalize in AI — and 20x in two years is not narrow progress.
